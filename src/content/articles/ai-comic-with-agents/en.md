The idea started as a short animation. A human arrives at work and finds three humanoid AI agents sitting at computers. Each one has a different role: **Implementer**, **Reviewer**, and **UX**. They comment on his lateness, appearance, and behavior with exaggerated machine-like precision.

The final exchange is:

> **Wait... why do you even have keyboards?**

And the answer:

> **It’s a legacy interface.**

The original plan was to create a simple video made of several scenes. In practice, the joke turned out not to need animation at all.

## From an idea to seven frames

The first step was to reduce the concept to a simple storyboard:

1. the human enters the office,
2. he sees three agents at their desks,
3. the Implementer comments on his lateness,
4. the Reviewer gives an absurdly precise measurement,
5. UX evaluates the missing tie,
6. the human notices the keyboards,
7. the punchline lands with “legacy interface”.

This made it easy to test whether the idea had a clear rhythm before generating anything.

## Dialogue as part of each agent’s personality

Each agent was given a slightly different speaking style.

The Implementer is direct:

> **He’s late.**

The Reviewer is excessively precise:

> **Exactly 20 minutes, 13 seconds, and 2 hundredths.**

UX evaluates the situation through a visual signal:

> **No tie. Low professional trust signal.**

The differences between the roles give the scene its character. The goal was not to portray AI realistically, but to create a light satire of narrow role-based analysis.

## Start with a reference frame

Instead of generating every image immediately, one wide reference frame came first.

It established:
- the office,
- the human character,
- the three AI agents,
- the color palette,
- the comic style,
- the role labels.

Only after that were the individual close-up frames generated.

That mattered because the main challenge in a sequence like this is not creating a single image. It is maintaining **visual consistency** across the whole story.

## Consistency matters more than extra effects

It would have been easy to focus on animation, camera movement, or transitions.

For this idea, the more important elements were simpler:
- the same characters,
- similar proportions,
- recognizable roles,
- a repeatable environment,
- readable dialogue.

If those stay consistent, the viewer immediately understands that each frame belongs to the same story.

## Why the comic worked better than animation

After the individual scenes were created, a single comic page was generated with the entire sequence.

At that point, the comic clearly worked better than the planned animation.

The reason was simple: the joke depends on sequence and timing, not motion. A static comic page:
- tells the whole story without editing,
- is easy to publish,
- works naturally inside an article,
- can be shared as a single image,
- keeps the concept lightweight.

It became a useful example of a format emerging during the creative process rather than being fixed at the beginning.

## AI as an iteration tool

AI was not used only to create the final image.

It supported several stages:
- expanding the original idea,
- simplifying the script,
- refining the dialogue,
- planning the storyboard,
- proposing a visual style,
- generating individual scenes,
- evaluating whether animation was necessary at all.

That is the more interesting part of the workflow. The value is not only in “generating an image”, but in moving quickly through multiple versions of the idea.

## What I learned

This small experiment highlights one practical rule:

> **Do not complicate the format when a simpler one communicates the idea better.**

At first, a short film seemed like the obvious direction. After a few iterations, the comic proved clearer, faster to consume, and better matched to the joke itself.

That kind of decision is a useful part of working with AI: the tool can help generate more options, but choosing the final form remains a human decision.
